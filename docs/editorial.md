# Historical editorial guide

1. Prefer original reports, specifications, proposals, creator accounts, and
   official release notes. Cite a section that supports the specific claim.
2. A language track begins at its first documented public release or publication.
   Identify which event is used. Record approximate years or intervals honestly.
3. Include significant semantic changes, feature adoptions, and substantial
   standards; do not list every patch release. Aim for 3–6 milestones per language.
4. Similar syntax does not prove influence. Every arrow needs evidence of an
   actual influence. Explain both adoption and adaptation. Mark disputed claims
   explicitly and explain the disagreement.
5. An influence can point to a destination milestone without identifying an exact
   source milestone. Such an arrow must not imply an exact source adoption date.
6. Cite both the destination's date and the relationship. Sources may document a
   broad family rather than one dialect: state that limitation. Language-family
   entries (notably Lisp and ML) are umbrellas, not assertions of compatibility.
7. Write original descriptions and small original examples. Linked documents keep
   their own licenses. No copied paragraphs or scraped copyrighted collections.
8. Missing arrows mean incomplete coverage, not evidence of no influence. Never
   infer a death date from an inactive implementation.
9. Wikipedia is welcome as a supplementary historical source and a route to
   original references. Cite the exact article and relevant section. Write an
   original explanation of the facts; Wikipedia prose is not relicensed by this
   repository. For specific influence claims, prefer a designer's account or
   language report when available.
